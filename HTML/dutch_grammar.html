📖 What is an Article? — Artikel
An article is a word placed before a noun to indicate whether it refers to something specific (definite) or non-specific (indefinite). In Dutch, every noun belongs to either the de-group or the het-group, and this must be memorised with each noun.

Definite Articlesde and het — used when referring to a specific, known noun. Like English "the". E.g. de deur (the door), het raam (the window).
Indefinite Articleeen — used when referring to any one instance of a noun, not a specific one. Like English "a / an". E.g. een deur (a door), een raam (a window).
No ArticleUsed when the noun is uncountable (cannot be counted). E.g. Ik drink water — no article needed before water or brood, for both de and het gender nouns.

Dutch has two definite articles (de and het) and one indefinite article (een). Knowing which article a noun takes is one of the most important — and must-memorise — aspects of Dutch.

de
Definite · Common Gender
Used with ~⅔ of all Dutch nouns — masculine and feminine nouns are both de-words. Also used for all plural nouns.
🔊 duh

het
Definite · Neuter Gender
Used with ~⅓ of Dutch nouns — neuter gender. Always singular. All diminutives ending in -je are always het-words.
🔊 het / uht

een
Indefinite · All Genders
Like English "a / an" — used for both de- and het-words. Not used with uncountable nouns (e.g. water, brood).
🔊 uhn (unstressed) / ayn (stressed)

💡 Key rule: You must memorise the article with each noun — there is no single perfect rule. Always learn together: de deur, het raam, de tafel…
All plurals use de, regardless of the singular article: het boek → de boeken.

👤 What is a Personal Pronoun? — Personaal Pronomen
A personal pronoun is a word that replaces a noun referring to a person or thing — as the subject of a sentence. Dutch personal pronouns come in two forms:

Stressed / EmphaticUsed regularly in writing, and in speech when the emphasis falls on the person. E.g. Jij doet het, niet ik. (YOU do it, not me.) Always safe to use — never incorrect.
UnstressedUsed in casual speech when the emphasis is on the verb, not the pronoun. E.g. je instead of jij; we instead of wij. To avoid confusion, use the stressed form.
Formal "u"Used as a polite form in formal sentences, with strangers, superiors, and people of an older generation. E.g. Woont u ook in Utrecht?

Personal pronouns have a stressed (emphatic) form and an unstressed form. The stressed form is always correct; the unstressed form is used when the emphasis is on the verb, not the pronoun.

💡 Formal "u": Used for strangers, superiors, and people of an older generation. In modern casual Dutch, jij / je is used among friends and peers.
Tip: To avoid confusion, use the stressed form — it is always correct.

🔑 What is a Possessive Pronoun? — Possessief Pronomen
A possessive pronoun indicates that something belongs to someone. Dutch has three ways to express possession, and possessive pronouns also have stressed and unstressed forms — just like personal pronouns.

Van (of)The regular Dutch way of showing possession using the word van with reference to a person or object. E.g. Het boek van Felicia (Felicia's book).
's suffixLike English, 's can be added to a noun. No apostrophe unless the noun ends in a vowel (other than e), or ends in s / x. E.g. Felicia's boek.
PronounUsing a possessive pronoun directly before the noun. E.g. Haar boek (her book). Like personal pronouns, these have stressed and unstressed forms: jouw (emphatic) vs je (normal use).
ons vs onzeSpecial rule for "our": use ons with singular het-words (e.g. ons boek), and onze with de-words or all plurals (e.g. onze docent, onze boeken).

Dutch has three ways to express possession: using van, adding 's to the noun, or using a possessive pronoun directly.

🎯 What is an Object Pronoun? — Object Pronomen
An object pronoun is used when the pronoun is the object of a verb — meaning the action is done to or for that person, not by them. Compare with the subject pronoun (the doer).

Subject → Objectik → mij / me  |  jij → jou / je  |  hij → hem  |  zij → haar. E.g. Kunt u mij helpen? (Can you help me?)
Stressed formUsed when the emphasis falls on the object. E.g. Hij heeft jou en mij in Utrecht gezien. (He saw you and me in Utrecht.)
DE-word as objectWhen referring back to a de-word thing (not a person), use hij (subject) or hem (object). E.g. de broek → Je kunt hem even passen. Hij staat je mooi.
HET-word as objectWhen referring back to a het-word thing, use het for both subject and object roles. E.g. het boek → Ik zie het niet. Het ligt niet op de tafel.
Plural (persons)Use hen or ze for people in the 3rd person plural object. E.g. Ik haal hen even uit de kantine.
Plural (things)Use ze for plural things (both de- and het-words). E.g. Ik hou van tomaten. Ik koop ze op de markt.

Object pronouns are used when the pronoun is the object of a verb — not the subject. Compare: Ik help jou — here jou is the object form of jij.

The stem of a verb = infinitive minus -en. For 2nd and 3rd person singular, add -t to the stem. Plural forms always use the full infinitive form.

Stemluisteren → luister  |  komen → kom
+ -t rule2nd & 3rd person singular always add -t to the stem: jij luistert, hij komt
PluralAll plural forms = full infinitive: wij / jullie / zij luisteren / komen

🔄
Inversion rule (jij / je): When jij / je comes after the verb (questions or inverted word order), the verb drops the -t: luistert → luister je | komt → kom je. Normal order keeps the -t: jij luistert.
⚠️
Exception — stem already ends in -t: If the verb stem itself ends in -t, no -t is removed during inversion — it belongs to the stem! zitten → stem zit → zit je / zit jij  |  heten → stem heet → heet je / heet jij

🔮 Modal Verbs — Modale Werkwoorden
Modal verbs complement another verb and can carry more than one meaning. The 5 Dutch modal verbs are: kunnen, mogen, moeten, willen, zullen.

Sentence structureThe modal verb is conjugated; the action verb appears as an infinitive at the end of the clause. E.g. Wat zullen we drinken?
Drop the infinitiveWhen the infinitive is obvious from context, it can be omitted. E.g. Ik wil graag koffie. (no need to say hebben)
Alternative formsFor jij/u: kunt / kan, wilt / wil, zult / zal are all correct.

The -t / -d Rule for Regular Participles

To form the participle of a regular verb: ge- + stem + -t or -d. Whether to use -t or -d depends on the final consonant of the stem.

Use -t after: S F K CH P T
Mnemonic: 't SoFt KeTChuP
If the verb stem ends in one of these voiceless consonants, the participle ends in -t.

werken → werk → ge + werk + t = gewerkt
fietsen → fiets → ge + fiets + t = gefietst
stoppen → stop → ge + stop + t = gestopt
Use -d after: all other consonants
If the stem does NOT end in a 't SoFt KeTChuP' consonant, the participle ends in -d.

spelen → speel → ge + speel + d = gespeeld
wonen → woon → ge + woon + d = gewoond
leven → leef → ge + leef + d = geleefd

✂️ Separable Verbs — Scheidbare Werkwoorden
Separable verbs are made of a preposition + verb that together create a completely new meaning. In many tenses the two parts split apart — the verb conjugates in position 2 while the preposition moves to the end of the sentence.

aankomenaan + komen → to arrive
instappenin + stappen → to board / get in
overstekenover + steken → to cross (the street)
meenemenmee + nemen → to take along
afrekenenaf + rekenen → to pay / settle the bill
tegenkomentegen + komen → to run into / encounter

How the verb splits — by tense

The behaviour of a separable verb depends on the tense. In every case, the preposition ends up at the end — except in the perfect where it fuses with ge-.

Present (Presens)
Verb conjugates normally; preposition goes to the end.

instappen:
Kirsten stapt bij het CS in.

meenemen:
Hij neemt de plattegrond mee.
Simple Past (Imperfectum)
Verb in simple past; preposition still goes to the end.

instappen:
Kirsten stapte bij het CS in.

meenemen (irregular):
Hij nam de plattegrond mee.
Present Perfect (Perfectum)
Participle = preposition + ge + verb stem + t/d. The whole thing goes to the end.

instappen → ingestapt
Hij is bij het CS ingestapt.

meenemen → meegenomen
Hij heeft de plattegrond meegenomen.
With Modal Verb
Modal is conjugated; infinitive of the full separable verb goes to the end — unsplit.

instappen:
U kunt direct instappen.

meenemen:
U mag de plattegrond meenemen.
With Hoeven + te
Hoeven requires te. With separable verbs, te goes between the preposition and the verb.

instappen:
U hoeft nog niet in te stappen.

meenemen:
U hoeft de plattegrond mee te nemen.
Imperative (Command)
In commands, the verb comes first; preposition goes to the end.

instappen:
Stap nog niet in!

meenemen:
Neemt u de plattegrond maar mee.

Forming the Participle of Separable Verbs

The ge- is inserted between the preposition and the verb stem. The result is one word.

Separable verb Base verb participle → Separable participle Regular / Irregular 🔊
✂️
Present / Simple Past — always split: The verb conjugates in position 2; the preposition moves to the very end. Hij neemt de plattegrond mee.
🔗
Perfect participle — preposition + ge + stem: The preposition stays glued to the front of the ge- participle, forming one word. instappen → ingestapt  |  meenemen → meegenomen
🔮

📐 Normal Sentence Structure — Normale Zin
Every normal Dutch sentence follows a fixed word order. The verb always holds the second position — this is one of the most fundamental rules of Dutch grammar.

SubjectThe doer of the action — a person, thing, or pronoun. E.g. Ik, De trein, Mijn vriend
VerbAlways the second element in a normal sentence. E.g. ga, fiets, studeer
RestEverything after the verb — in a fixed internal order of Time → Manner → Place

The "Rest": Time → Manner → Place

When the rest contains multiple elements, they follow a strict order: first when, then how, then where. This is often remembered as T–M–P (Tijd – Manier – Plaats).

⏰ Time (Tijd)
When? — comes first in the Rest.

morgen — tomorrow
na werk — after work
in de ochtend — in the morning
de hele dag — the whole day
🚶 Manner (Manier)
How? — comes second in the Rest.

met de trein — by train
door de regen — through the rain
met mijn medecursisten — with my classmates
snel — quickly
📍 Place (Plaats)
Where? — always comes last in the Rest.

naar het park — to the park
naar de examenlocatie — to the exam location
naar huis — home
op school — at school

🔄 Inversie — Inverted Word Order — Inversie
In a normal sentence the subject comes first. In inversie, something other than the subject is placed at the front — and the verb still stays in position 2. The subject is then pushed to position 3, right after the verb.

PersoonsvormThe conjugated verb in the sentence — always at position 2, regardless of what comes first. In Dutch this is called the persoonsvorm.

📚 Dutch Plurals — Pluralis / Meervoud
Dutch plurals fall into three main categories. There is no single perfect rule — the ending depends on the word's spelling and stress pattern. Plurals always take the article de.

-en endingThe most common plural ending. Used for most nouns. E.g. tomaat → tomaten, boodschap → boodschappen
-s endingUsed for words ending in an unstressed syllable (l, m, n, r, el, em, en, er, je) and foreign loanwords. E.g. buitenlander → buitenlanders, champignon → champignons
's endingUsed when the noun ends in a vowel a, i, o, u, or y — an apostrophe is added before the s. E.g. paprika → paprika's, auto → auto's
IrregularSome nouns have completely irregular plural forms that must be memorised. E.g. kind → kinderen, ei → eieren, stad → steden
No pluralMeasurements, time units, and currencies often have no plural form: cent, jaar, uur, euro, kilo, kilometer

The Four Categories

① Plural -en
Most common ending. Spelling rules may apply (double consonant or shortened vowel).

het boontje → de boontjes

③ Plural -'s (apostrophe)
When noun ends in a, i, o, u, y — add apostrophe + s.

de paprika → de paprika's
de kiwi → de kiwi's
de auto → de auto's
de menu → de menu's
de hobby → de hobby's
④ Irregular Plurals
No rule — must be memorised!

de stad → de steden
het kind → de kinderen
het ei → de eieren

Plural Forms at a Glance

Singular Plural Category 🔊

No Plural Form

Measurements, time units, and currencies use the same form for singular and plural.

💡 These nouns have no plural: cent, jaar, uur, euro, kilo, kilometer, liter, meter — and other units of measurement, time, length, weight, and liquid volume.
📌
All plurals use de: Regardless of the singular article (de or het), all plural nouns take de. E.g. het kind → de kinderen  |  het boontje → de boontjes
🔤
-s for unstressed syllables: If the last syllable of the noun is unstressed and ends in l, m, n, r, or the noun ends in -je, use -s. Also applies to foreign loanwords like champignons, cafés.
'
Apostrophe before -s for vowel endings: If the noun ends in a, i, o, u, y, always write 's — not just s. This prevents misreading the vowel. auto → auto's ✓  (not autos)
